ALFONSO GORDON CABELLO DE LOS COBOS

I’m a biomedical engineer who builds technology for movement and rehabilitation. My work sits where biomechanics, robotics, VR, and AI meet, and it’s driven by one question: how can engineering make recovery more effective, and more engaging, for the people going through it? That has meant building a VR escape room with LLM-powered characters that physically adjusts a gait rehabilitation robot for children with cerebral palsy, and designing a statistical shape analysis tool to help plan patient-specific bone reconstruction. I recently completed my MSc in Biomedical Engineering at Brown University, and I’m now looking for R&D and Field service engineer roles in medical devices, rehabilitation robotics or digital health, in Europe and the US.

RESEARCH EXPERIENCE

Brown University: Department of Orthopaedics

When a wrist bone is damaged, surgeons need a good template for reconstruction. I analyzed 120 CT-derived scaphoid models from 90 subjects and built a standalone MATLAB application that aligns 3D bones automatically and runs statistical shape analysis. The result: a person’s own opposite hand is a far better template (mean Hausdorff distance of 1.44 mm, 97.47% volumetric similarity) than a bone from another person (median Hausdorff distance above 3 mm). In other words, the healthy contralateral hand is the best guide for precise, patient-specific reconstruction.

BioRobotics Group

At the Spanish National Research Council’s robotics center I worked on Discover2Walk, a robotic gait rehabilitation platform. As an intern, I built a web interface that lets clinicians safely configure rehabilitation sessions, save patient data, and compare a patient’s walking against a theoretical model in real time using motion cameras.

As a research assistant, I went further and built a VR serious game (an escape room) with LLM-driven characters, connected to the robot through ROS2. In-game hazards change the patient’s walking speed, and the conversations create a record for analyzing cognitive progress in children with cerebral palsy. I presented this work at the Symposium on Robotics, Bioengineering and Computer Vision in Badajoz (in Spanish) and co-authored the accompanying paper.

EDUCATION

I chose a master’s degree to fill the gaps I found after my bachelor’s, and Brown’s flexibility let me build exactly the toolkit I wanted. Universidad CEU-San Pablo

Biomedical Engineering BSc

My bachelor’s degree is where I learned to think like an engineer and found out what kind of engineer I wanted to be. The program covered a wide range, from anatomy and instrumentation to programming, signal processing, and CAD, and I enjoyed that breadth because the problems I care about sit between disciplines. What I valued most was how often the coursework turned into real projects, whether designing a component for a pediatric exoskeleton, building software with a team, or developing a VR world. Those projects taught me that technical correctness is only half the job, and that a device or tool also has to work for the person using it.

Outside the classroom, I served as class representative for three years and as the bachelor’s representative on the Undergraduate Student Council. Speaking for my classmates taught me to listen carefully and to turn a general frustration into a proposal people could act on. In my third year, I also spent a year abroad on ERASMUS at Lodz University of Technology in Poland, which showed me another way of teaching engineering and pushed me to become more independent and adaptable. I finished my degree with an undergraduate thesis on gait analysis for rehabilitation robotics, which set the direction for much of my work afterwards.

The certificate gave me the tools to pursue that idea seriously.
